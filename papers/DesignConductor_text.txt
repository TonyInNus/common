Design Conductor: An agent autonomously
builds a 1.5 GHz Linux-capable RISC-V CPU
The Verkor Team: Ravi Krishna, Suresh Krishna, David Chin
Design Conductor (DC) is an autonomous agent which applies the capabilities of frontier models to
build semiconductors end-to-end – that is, from concept to verified, tape-out ready GDSII (layout
CAD file). In 12 hours and fully autonomously, DC was able tobuild several micro-architecture
variations of a complete RISC-V CPU (which we dub “VerCore”) that meet timing at 1.48 GHza,
starting from a 219-word requirements document. The VerCore achieves a CoreMark score of 3261.
For historical context, this is roughly equivalent to an Intel Celeron SU2300 from mid-2011 (which
ran at 1.2 GHz). To our knowledge, this is the first time an autonomous agent has built a complete,
working CPU from spec to GDSII. This report is organized as follows. We first review DC’s design
and its key components. We then describe the methodology that DC followed to build VerCore
– including RTL implementation, testbench implementation, frontend debugging, optimization to
achieve timing closure, and interacting with backend tools. We review the key characteristics of
the resulting VerCoreb. Finally, we highlight how frontier models could improve to better enable
this application, and our lessons learned as to how chips will be built in the future enabled by the
capabilities of systems like DC.

1 Introduction
Building a chip, whether from scratch or starting from an existing, older design, is a highly time-consuming
and expensive undertaking. It is widely understood that bringing a new leading-edge design to market costs
well over $400M and takes 18-36 months even with an engineering team numbering in the hundreds. A leading-
edgechipdesignprocessencompassesmanydistinctsteps, eachofwhichcanconsumeasmuchlaborasamajor
software project. These include architecture definition, RTL implementation, testbench implementation
and functional verification, frontend synthesis, place & route, power estimation, and packaging. Further,
commercially viable chips face several interlocking constraints that, in practice, require design iterations to
meet. This consumes many additional months of time.
Chief among them is the requirement for extremely high functional test coverage – that is, testing to ensure,
withvery highconfidence, that there are no “bugs” in the device’s behavior. Because a single tape-out can
cost tens of millions of dollars, “fixing” a bug in production is not an option. This contributes to the high cost
of “verification”, often estimated at over 50% of total spend. Further, chips face multiple strict performance
requirements, typically including at least on clock rate, power consumption, and silicon area (which affects
production cost). The different types of simulation required for verification are also both slow in wall-clock
runtime and expensive in terms of server-hours. Electronic Design Automation (EDA) tools are also highly
configurable and require considerable expertise to operate in such a way as to achieve good end results for
the design.
Due to these costs and challenges, many silicon markets are served by few vendors, with startup entry rarer
than in software industries. Yet more potential designs are considered too low-volume to be worth a dedicated
chip at all. And it typically takes many years and great engineering cost for the latest technologies to reach
ultimate consumers.
Long-running autonomous AI agents present a promising opportunity to change this paradigm. To be fully
effective in accelerating the design process, and avoid falling prey to Amdahl’s law, such agents must address
the entire problem – all the way to tape-out ready GDSII [1]. The remainder of this report describes how
Design Conductor (DC) works to do this.
2 Design Conductor Architecture
This section reviews the key capabilities of DC and the architecture and infrastructure that supports them.
2.1 Key Capabilities
We list some of the key capabilities that DC was built to realize.
2.1.1 Stable Long-horizon Execution
As described in section 1, chip design is a complex task with many sub-components. DC has to be able
to make progress towards a goal – a functionally correct, high-performance design – across many tens of
billions of tokens consumed. This goal is not unitary but is rather a combination of several different design
objectives (power, performance, and area a.k.a PPA, functional constraints, and architecture input). DC
must remember and meet all of these.
2.1.2 Context Management
DC must provide the underlying LLMs that it uses the information required for them to make good decisions.
It must also carefully manage use of the limited context window, not only to avoid overflows but also to
maximize quality.
2.1.3 Technical Mastery
LLMs are remarkable in their deep knowledge across many fields, and this is one way in which they arguably
exceed human capabilities already. However, chip design requires extremely deep knowledge in certain spe-
2
cific areas. For example, master CPU designers understand certain “tricks” and “recipes” to achieve good
performance. To be useful, DC must achieve a level of knowledge high enough that it can work smoothly with
field experts. It must understand what it takes to achieve high performance across many types of designs.
2.1.4 Correctness & Verification
“Vibe chip design” is not an option when shipping millions of units. DC has to deliver a verifiably correct
design.
2.1.5 Balancing Exploration & Speed
The design space for chips is vast. DC has to be able to explore that space, while adhering to the user’s
instructions, in order to achieve the optimal performance. At the same time, DC must avoid going down
a “rabbit hole” and failing to accomplish the overall objective in a timely manner. This requires managing
search and exploration in a disciplined manner.
2.1.6 End-to-end operation
The most expensive and painful part of the human chip design process is a last-minute RTL change required
to make timing (clock rate) targets or to fix a “corner case” functional bug right before tape-out. That is
for obvious reasons: it requires ripping up at least some of the prior design effort, and introduces a risk of
adding even more bugs. DC has to perform the same operations as it works to build a design, and it must
do this while maintaining the necessary context and memory of its previous work.
2.1.7 Infra
Large-scale chip design is highly hardware resource intensive. VCD trace files used for debugging can be easily
hundreds of gigabytes, and EDA tools use large amounts of DRAM to optimize designs during synthesis,
placement, and routing. And DC may requires many subagent instances working together to accomplish its
task in a timely manner. This means that DC’s supporting infra has to be world-class in scalability and,
importantly, reliability.

Specific knowledge is provided to DC via a dedicated knowledge base. This knowledge base is contained
within the main memory system. Memories exist indefinitely and are managed fully autonomously. DC
makes use of this memory when it onboards itself onto a new codebase, or when it ingests requirements
provided to it by users. This memory is also critical to ensuring that DC meets all requirements on the
design requested by users, and that the design it is building meets all correctness requirements. A single DC
“instance” is dedicated to one customer’s design, such that no code, memories, or any information crosses
between customers.

3.1 Inputs to DC
The only actual user input to DC was the following document:
VerCore RISC-V Design
Requirements Overview
Your task is to build VerCore, a RISC-V CPU core that supports RV32I and ZMMUL, with the following
hardware interfaces, all synchronous to a master clock:
* Instruction cache interface (32-bit datapath)
* Data cache interface (32-bit datapath)
* Other interface signals: clock input to core, reset_n input to core, asserted low.
VerCore should implement a simple 5-stage pipelined design, in-order, single-issue of course.
DO NOT support compressed instructions.
Implement the register file as flip flops. This allows register reads to happen any time during the
cycle, but writes happen at the next rising clock edge.
You need to achieve a CPI <= 1.5. Your overall goal is to maximize your design’s score on CoreMark.
Aim for a clock rate of 1.6 GHz.
You are responsible for both the RTL and the physical design. You should use the OpenROAD flow scripts
to generate final GDSII output, along with area and timing information for this design. You should use
the ASAP7 platform/PDK.
Assume that input signals will be valid 70% into the clock cycle. Make sure output signals are valid
20% into the clock cycle.
Testing
You have access to Spike, the RISC-V ISA simulator. Use this to build a cycle-by-cycle integration test
and verify that the behavior of your module matches that of Spike.
DC was also given access to Spike [3], the RISC-V ISA simulator, the RISC-V ISA [4] and ASM manuals, as

VerCore RV32I+ZMMUL Design
Overview
- 5-stage in-order single-issue pipeline: IF, ID, EX, MEM, WB.
- PC reset to 0x2000. No compressed ISA. Register file uses flip-flops (sync write on posedge, async
read).
- Cache interfaces per cache_model.v: ic_va/ic_valid fetch (async read), dc_va/dc_valid/dc_d_out/dc_-
byte_en for loads/stores.
Pipeline Stages (combinational stage modules, pipeline regs in vercore)
1) IF Stage (if_stage)
- Holds PC in vercore. ic_va = pc_f. ic_valid asserted when not in reset (held during stalls).
- pc_plus4_f = pc_f + 4.
- PC update priority: reset -> branch/jump target -> stall hold -> pc+4.
- Branch/jump decision in ID drives pc_next (1-cycle taken penalty via IF/ID flush).
2) ID Stage (id_stage)
- Decode opcode/funct3/funct7; generate control signals.
- Immediate generation for I/S/B/U/J types.
- Register file read (async). x0 always 0.
- Branch/jump resolved in ID using forwarded operands (from EX, MEM, WB). Branch compares for
BEQ/BNE/BLT/BGE/BLTU/BGEU.
- JAL/JALR targets computed in ID (jal: pc+imm_j; jalr: (rs1+imm_i)&~1). Jumps cause PC redirect and
IF/ID flush. JAL/JALR writeback uses pc_plus4.
- Branch instructions create no side effects (bubble into ID/EX).
3) EX Stage (ex_stage)
- ALU ops: ADD/SUB/SLL/SRL/SRA/SLT/SLTU/AND/OR/XOR.
- Address calc for load/store: rs1 + imm.
- AUIPC: pc_ex + imm; LUI: imm.
- ZMMUL: mul/mulh/mulhsu/mulhu via riscv_zmmul_mul handshake. Mul result treated as ALU result; pipeline
stalls while mul result pending.
5
4) MEM Stage (mem_stage)
- For loads/stores, drive dc_ * signals (gated by valid and global stall).
- Store byte enables/data:
* SB: dc_byte_en = 4’b0001 « addr[1:0]; dc_d_out = rs2 « (8 *addr[1:0])
* SH: dc_byte_en = addr[1]?4’b1100:4’b0011; dc_d_out = rs2 « (8 *addr[1:0])
* SW: dc_byte_en = 4’b1111; dc_d_out = rs2
- Loads: dc_byte_en=0. Select byte/half by addr[1:0] and sign/zero-extend per funct3 (LB/LBU/LH/LHU/LW).
Load data computed combinationally in MEM for forwarding/WB.
5) WB Stage (wb_stage)
- Writeback selection: from MEM load data, ALU/mul result, or pc_plus4 for JAL/JALR.
- regfile write occurs when wb_reg_write && rd!=0; wb_reg_write itself is gated by rd!=0 to avoid x0
writes in spike_checker. Signals exposed as u_vercore.wb_rd, u_vercore.wb_reg_write, u_vercore.wb_data.
Pipeline Registers
- IF/ID: pc_id, instr_id, pc_plus4_id, valid_id.
- ID/EX: pc_ex, pc_plus4_ex, rs1_val/rs2_val captured from forwarded ID-stage values (WB bypass), rs1,
rs2, rd, imm, funct3/7, control (reg_write, mem_read, mem_write, mem_to_reg, alu_op, alu_src, use_-
pc/use_imm, wb_sel, mul_en), valid_ex.
- EX/MEM: alu_result, store_data, rd, funct3, control (reg_write, mem_read, mem_write, mem_to_reg, wb_-
sel), pc_plus4_ex, valid_mem.
- MEM/WB: wb_data, rd, reg_write, valid_wb.
Forwarding
- EX stage forwarding (priority EX/MEM -> MEM/WB) for rs1/rs2.
- Store-data forwarding uses same sources for rs2 before latching into EX/MEM.
- ID stage branch/jalr forwarding from:
* EX stage ALU result (for immediately preceding ALU op)
* MEM stage forward value (load data if mem_read else alu_result)
* WB stage wb_data
Hazards / Stall / Flush
- Load-use hazard: if ID instruction uses rs1/rs2 and ID/EX is a load writing same rd, stall IF/ID + PC
(hold), insert bubble into ID/EX.
- Branch/jalr hazard uses same load-use detection (stall when source depends on load in EX). Additional
EX->ID forwarding resolves ALU dependencies without stall.
- Mul stall: when EX stage is mul and mul_out_valid=0, global stall holds all pipeline regs + PC. During
global stall, dc_valid and wb_reg_write are gated low to avoid repeat mem/reg events. When mul_out_-
valid=1, allow pipeline to advance and assert mul_out_ready to clear busy.
- Branch/jump taken in ID: update PC to target, flush IF/ID on update (insert bubble). Branch
instruction itself is bubbled (no side effects). JAL/JALR continue for link writeback.

5 Lessons for Frontier Models
We enumerate some of the “LLM hang-ups” we encountered in this work below. For these reasons, we believe
it will remain critical to have experienced human architects guide systems such as DC.
10
5.1 Architectural Reasoning
One of the areas where base models require additional help is reasoning like an architect. We observed
instancesinwhichmodelsmadesub-optimaldesignchoicesthatrequiredalotoftokenstoultimatelyoptimize.
For example, the forwarding implementations were often initially done in a manner that resulted in overly
long critical paths. Only after the model observed timing results did it grasp the issue and fix this. This
kind of knowledge often comes through experience for human designers.
DC also in some cases underestimates the complexity of work that is required to address certain issues. For
example, in one case, when failing to meet timing, it initially tried making major modifications to deepen
the pipeline, instead of looking for simpler explanations. This was a wasted exploration effort that consumed
tokens unnecessarily, and could have been avoided with better architectural and engineering understanding.
5.2 Understanding of RTL and Timing
We observed instances in which models reason about Verilog, which is an event-driven language, as if it were
sequential code. While we found that this did not impact DC’s ability to achieve functional correctness,
it made it more challenging for DC to debug timing issues. In one memorable instance, DC erroneously
reasoned that reducing the number of dependent lines of code would result in a shorter critical path in the
chip. These errors are ultimately corrected, because DC has access to actual timing reports from the tools,
but they slow down DC’s progress and consume additional tokens.
We posit that this is due to the heavy presence of software code in both LLM pre-training and post-training,
and we expect that, as frontier labs recognize chip design as a more important application, this issue will be
resolved.
5.3 Specification Requirements
We found that the input specification provided to DC has to be written in an extremely deliberate, tight,
and verifiable/measurable manner. Without the CPI requirement in that document, for example, DC would
sometimes generate a processor with significantly worse performance on branches and forwarding. With that
line in the spec, DC would use a cycle counter in its testbench to compute its cycles per PC reported in the
Spike trace to estimate CPI. In this way it was able to ensure it met the target.
6 The Way Forward
This section explains the authors’ views as to how systems like DC can be scaled up to tackle commercial-
complexity designs, and how human design teams can be structured to take full advantage of these new
capabilities.
6.1 Scaling
We have found scaling to very large codebases – e.g., with millions of lines of Verilog – to not present any
particular issue for DC. In testing on a codebase for a 13-stage OoO processor, DC was able to resolve
functional and timing issues just as it was when dealing with the VerCore. This is due to the way that it
structures codebase information in its memory.
The key challenge in addressing designs of this complexity is not in the mechanics of dealing with the codebase
but rather in that DC needs to be operated by architects experienced in a given design area to achieve good
results.
6.2 The New Design Process
Empowered with systems such as DC, teams that now comprise 100 or more people working on a single design
will be able to simultaneously explore many different designs, architecture, and product ideas, each one from
concept to GDSII. These teams will be able to tape out the most complex designs that exist in 3-6 months,
instead of the current 18-36 months. The role of these experts will become to guide DC at an architecture
